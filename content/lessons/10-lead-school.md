## You don't need permission to start

You don't need to be class president, or an "expert", or 18. You need: a clear cause, a small team, and consistency.

### Step 1 — Form a tiny core team (3-5 people)

Recruit friends who actually care. A group of 3 *committed* students is more powerful than 30 vaguely interested.

### Step 2 — Get a teacher sponsor

A supportive science / geography / civics teacher gives you:

- Access to a classroom for meetings.
- Help getting school-administration approval.
- Credibility with parents.

### Step 3 — Set a 1-semester goal

Pick **one** measurable target. Examples:

- 1 cleanup per month.
- 50 TideGuard reports filed by the team.
- 5 lessons completed by every team member by end of semester.

### Step 4 — Run a launch event

- 1 information meeting (20 min) for interested students.
- 1 first cleanup (90 min) within 3 weeks.
- 1 short follow-up post on the school's Instagram with photos.

### Step 5 — Document and share

Photos, kg collected, before/after maps, certificates of completion. Submit your school to the TideGuard **/leaderboard** to compete with other schools regionally.

### Step 6 — Hand off

Train a younger student to take your place when you graduate. The biggest leadership failure is when a club dies because the founder leaves. Build the system to outlive you.

## Public speaking, briefly

- Tell a *story*, not statistics. "Last Saturday we picked up 23 kg of plastic. Half of it was straws and bottle caps. One of the bottles still had a label in a language we couldn't read — it came from across the strait."
- Always end with **one ask**: "Join our next cleanup", "Use a refillable bottle this week", "Submit one report".


```task
- 30-minute mini-task: pick one street, river or beach near you and walk it. Photograph any plastic item bigger than a coin, then log it via the TideGuard app.
- Goal: at least 5 reports with photo + GPS within 30 minutes.
- Wrap-up: write 2 sentences in your notebook describing what surprised you.
```
